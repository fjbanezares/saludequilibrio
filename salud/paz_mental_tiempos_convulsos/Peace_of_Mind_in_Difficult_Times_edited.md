# Peace of Mind in Difficult Times

*Edited transcript of a Buddhist talk. Greetings, venue-specific remarks, promotional material, spoken-language filler and unnecessary repetition have been removed. The substance and sequence of the talk have been preserved, while the two meditation practices are separated from the main teaching.*

---

## Part I — Opening Meditation

Sit in a comfortable position and relax physically and mentally.

Breathe naturally, preferably through the nostrils rather than the mouth. Become aware of the sensation of the breath as it enters and leaves the nostrils. Let this sensation be the object of meditation and try to focus on it to the exclusion of everything else.

There will be a strong temptation to follow distracting thoughts as they arise. Try to resist this temptation and keep your attention on the sensation of the breath.

If you notice that your attention has wandered, stop following the distracting thought and gently return your attention to the breath. You may need to do this many times. Eventually the mind begins to settle.

As distracting thoughts subside, a sense of relaxation and mental peace arises. Rest in this state of calm for a short while.

With this more peaceful mind, develop a good motivation for listening:

> May I hear something helpful and beneficial that I can put into practice, so that I can improve myself, become a better person, and become increasingly able to benefit others.

Resolve to listen with a clear mind and a good heart, and to try not to forget what is useful.

Then gently conclude the breathing meditation.

# Part II — The Teaching

## 1. Inner peace and world peace

Even after only a few minutes of meditation, it is possible to notice a degree of peace in the mind. This is important because world peace cannot develop without peace developing first within individual minds.

The cultivation of inner peace is therefore not merely personal relaxation. It is connected with the way we live, the way we relate to other people and, ultimately, with peace in the wider world.

A simple practice such as breathing meditation can be useful every day, especially for someone new to meditation. When the mind is overwhelmed by distracting thoughts, even a short period of calm can make it easier to think clearly.

The deeper point, however, is that meditation is not limited to following the breath. Breathing meditation is a preparation. It settles the mind so that we can train in ways of thinking that change how we experience our life.

## 2. What meditation is

Meditation is a mind that focuses single-pointedly on a virtuous object and whose function is to make the mind peaceful and calm.

For practical purposes, a virtuous object is something that produces a more peaceful and constructive state of mind.

The purpose of meditation is therefore to make the mind calm and peaceful. If our mind is peaceful, we experience happiness. If our mind is not peaceful, it is very difficult to be happy even when our external conditions are excellent.

We already know this from experience.

We can be in a pleasant restaurant, with someone we love, eating food we enjoy. Then something happens and we become annoyed. Suddenly the enjoyment disappears. The restaurant has not changed. The food has not changed. What has changed is our mind.

A peaceful and positive state of mind has become negative, and our own happiness has disappeared with it.

This happens because we have strong mental habits: anger, jealousy, pride, greed, selfishness and confusion. If we learn to work with these habits, we can gradually train the mind to remain peaceful even when circumstances are difficult.

## 3. Worldly pleasure and real happiness

When distracting thoughts settle and the mind becomes still, a feeling of peace, contentment and well-being can arise from within.

This is different from ordinary worldly pleasure.

Worldly pleasure depends heavily on external conditions. The happiness that comes from a peaceful mind arises internally. It is therefore far more useful when life becomes difficult.

Much of the stress and tension we experience comes from the mind, and this stress can make many other problems harder to deal with.

Even ten or fifteen minutes of breathing meditation each day can reduce mental agitation and produce a calmer, more spacious feeling.

This inner calm is important because it is extremely difficult to find good solutions to difficult situations while the mind is disturbed and negative.

With a calmer mind we also tend to feel warmer and better disposed towards other people, which gradually improves our relationships.

## 4. Difficult times are not an exception

We often imagine that our real happiness will begin when difficult times finally stop.

But difficult times do not seem to disappear permanently. They become more intense, then less intense, and then another difficulty appears.

If someone asked us to name two or three important difficulties in our life right now, most of us would not need much time to think.

Sometimes we imagine that we are the only ones struggling. Social media can reinforce this impression because other people's lives often appear happier and easier than our own.

But everyone experiences difficulties: rich and poor, young and old. Aging, sickness and death are also part of the human condition.

Acknowledging this means looking honestly at our situation.

The question is therefore not:

**How can I construct a life in which nothing difficult ever happens?**

A more useful question is:

**How can I keep a peaceful mind when difficulties happen?**

## 5. Make suffering meaningful

One of the most important ideas in the teaching is:

> Make your suffering meaningful.

Difficulties will come anyway. If they can be used to produce something beneficial, then they no longer have to be meaningless experiences that simply make us unhappy.

Two simple analogies illustrate this.

### The olive farmer

Most people would not be excited if someone offered them a pile of sheep manure. But an organic olive farmer may see exactly the same thing as useful fertilizer.

The object is the same. Its value changes according to the way it is understood and used.

### The football player

If you are casually playing with a football and someone runs at you and takes the ball, you may become irritated.

But if you are training to become a better football player, someone who challenges you and makes it difficult for you to keep possession is helping you improve.

Again, the same kind of difficulty can be experienced very differently depending on the mind.

The point is not that difficulties are pleasant. The point is that they can become useful.

## 6. We cannot eliminate every difficulty, but we can change our response

We will never create an ordinary life that contains no difficulties.

It does not matter where we move, who we live with or what job we do. Problems continue to arise.

The Buddhist approach is therefore to learn how to use difficulties.

This becomes especially important when large difficulties appear: bereavement, a serious diagnosis, divorce, redundancy, physical pain or mental pain.

Major difficulties often humble us. They challenge the feeling that we are completely in control of our life.

At such times deeper questions can arise:

1. What is the real meaning of my life?
2. What will make me truly happy?
3. What will enable me to benefit others most?
4. What will help me when my life is drawing to a close?

The teaching gives one basic answer to all four:

**Inner development.**

We can also call it spiritual development, or simply improving the mind.

## 7. The real meaning of inner development

To develop inwardly means to become more patient, loving, kind, compassionate and wise.

The aim is to become the best person we can become, gradually improving week by week, month by month and year by year.

As inner peace increases, we discover that a stable kind of happiness comes with it.

This changes the usual direction in which we search for happiness.

Most of us have spent our lives trying to obtain happiness mainly from external conditions. But the teaching proposes that the real source of happiness is inner peace.

Happiness is the experience of a peaceful mind.

If the mind is peaceful, happiness can arise even when external circumstances are difficult. If the mind is deeply disturbed, even excellent external circumstances may fail to make us happy.

This does not mean abandoning work, family, money, homes or ordinary responsibilities.

We continue with our external life while simultaneously developing the mind.

## 8. Difficulties can become the material of inner development

If our aim is to develop the mind, then an important question appears:

**What will help us become more patient, humble, compassionate and wise?**

One answer is:

**Our difficulties.**

Difficulties can be used to develop the very qualities that make the mind stronger and more peaceful.

Instead of beginning with the largest suffering in our life, it is better to choose a relatively small difficulty and ask:

> What can this difficulty teach me?

This changes the relationship we have with adversity.

We are no longer only trying to get rid of the experience. We are also asking how it can contribute to our development.

## 9. The situation is not always the problem

When things go wrong, we normally identify the external situation itself as the problem.

We think: *This is my problem.*

But according to the teaching, the painful problem we experience depends greatly on the way our mind responds to the situation.

If we could respond to a difficult circumstance with a positive and peaceful mind, it would affect us very differently.

It might even be experienced as a challenge or an opportunity for growth.

Problems become much more painful when we respond with anger, frustration, disappointment, discouragement, anxiety or unhappiness.

These negative states make the situation harder to deal with while simultaneously destroying the peace of mind that is the source of our happiness.

So if we want to be happy more consistently, we need to learn to develop and maintain a peaceful mind.

This is a skill.

# Part III — Five Ways to Transform Adversity

## 10. Humility

Difficulty can develop humility.

When life is going well, it is easy to feel that we understand what we are doing and that events are under our control.

Then something happens that was never part of our plan.

Difficulty can make us recognize the limits of our understanding.

This humility is valuable because it opens the mind to learning.

The more humility we have, the more we can learn from people, situations and experience.

Pride tends to close this process down.

Difficulty, therefore, can become a teacher of humility.

## 11. Patience

Patience is often misunderstood.

It does not mean becoming increasingly irritated while telling ourselves that we are being patient.

Real patience is the ability to accept a difficult situation in order to preserve peace of mind—our own and that of others.

This is important because inner peace is the source of happiness.

When adversity appears, anger destroys that peace. Acceptance protects it.

If something difficult is already happening, refusing mentally to accept the reality of it does not make it disappear. It often adds another layer of suffering.

Sickness is happening. A relationship has ended. Someone has criticized us. We are experiencing physical or mental pain.

The first fact is the difficult situation itself. The second problem is the mind fighting against the fact that it is happening.

Acceptance does not mean saying that the situation is good. It means recognizing reality without destroying our peace of mind in the process.

We can think:

> This difficulty is already here. I will accept that it is here, preserve my peace of mind, and decide wisely what to do next.

Start with small situations.

Someone says something unpleasant at work. Instead of immediately becoming angry, recognize that these are words and that the other person may themselves be upset.

If the mind remains peaceful, that is already a victory.

With practice, patience becomes an inner strength.

Anger is not strength simply because it feels forceful. Anyone can become angry. It takes much greater strength to remain peaceful in a difficult situation.

Patience is therefore a form of inner wealth.

External wealth cannot guarantee happiness and cannot be taken with us when we die. Inner qualities stay with and shape the mind.

Difficulties give us repeated opportunities to develop that wealth.

## 12. Understanding karma

Another way of responding to difficulty is through the Buddhist teaching on karma.

We often ask:

**Why is this happening to me?**

Interestingly, we rarely ask the same question when everything is going well.

The teaching on karma proposes that our experiences have causes.

Virtuous actions are causes of happiness and non-virtuous actions are causes of suffering.

Living in accordance with karma therefore means trying to avoid actions that harm others and engaging as much as possible in actions that benefit them.

From this perspective, when something difficult happens we can think of it as the ripening of previous karmic potential.

Instead of reacting with anger or despair, we try to keep a calm and peaceful mind and allow the experience to pass.

The practical emphasis is important: reacting negatively to difficulty creates a mental environment in which the experience becomes even harder to bear.

The training is therefore to begin with smaller difficulties and learn to remain constructive and peaceful.

## 13. Compassion

Difficulty can also become a reminder of other people's suffering.

Normally, when we are suffering, nearly all our attention contracts around our own problem.

The teaching encourages the opposite movement.

If we are seriously ill, for example, we can remember all the people in our city, our country and the world who are experiencing the same illness or something even worse.

We can think:

> May they be free from pain and suffering.

That mind is compassion.

Instead of remaining completely absorbed in ourselves, suffering becomes a reminder of the suffering of others.

Even a migraine or back pain can be used in this way.

Ask:

**Which is more important—the suffering of one person or the suffering of many people?**

If we could relieve the suffering of ten, one hundred or countless people, their suffering would obviously matter enormously.

So when we are the one suffering, we can consciously expand the mind beyond ourselves:

> Countless others are experiencing difficulties greater than mine. May they be free from their pain and suffering.

In this way, our own difficulty becomes material for developing compassion.

## 14. Everything depends on how we look at it

The final method is deeper.

A situation appears adverse because of the way we identify and interpret it.

If we continually think, *This is terrible. This should not be happening. This is ruining my life*, then the situation is experienced entirely as adversity.

But if we learn to think, *This difficulty is helping me grow. It is helping me develop patience, humility, compassion and strength of mind*, then the same situation begins to function differently.

We change our attitude by changing the way we recognize the experience.

The teaching gives an example of two people with the same illness.

One transforms the illness into a positive spiritual experience using methods such as patience and compassion. The other does not.

The first may remain cheerful even while experiencing severe pain. The second may become increasingly depressed and anxious.

We often know people who have faced great difficulty with extraordinary courage, faith, patience, love or compassion. They become an inspiration to others.

The aim of this training is to become such a person.

## 15. Transforming adversity into the spiritual path

Using difficulties to develop good qualities has a traditional name:

**Transforming adversity into the spiritual path.**

As we become more skilled at this, fear can gradually diminish.

The reason is not that bad things are guaranteed to stop happening.

The reason is that whatever happens can increasingly be used for development.

This makes us stronger internally.

We become more capable of bearing difficulty with a peaceful, wise and compassionate mind.

We may also become someone other people can rely on.

People sometimes say that another person is “my rock.” But if two people are both drowning, neither can support the other very effectively.

Developing inner strength enables us to be of greater benefit to others.

## 16. Peace of mind as a daily practice

The central practice can be summarized very simply.

During ordinary life—at work, at home, while dealing with relationships, responsibilities and unexpected events—we try to develop and maintain peace of mind.

When a difficulty comes, instead of thinking only, *How do I get rid of this?*, we can also ask:

*How can I use this?*

Can it teach humility?

Can it train patience?

Can it remind me to live more carefully and constructively?

Can it deepen compassion?

Can it help me change the way I look at events?

The aim is not to remain the same person year after year.

Every difficulty can become part of the process of improving the mind.

## 17. From personal peace to world peace

It is easy to look at the state of the world and feel hopeless.

But the teaching returns to the same principle:

> Without inner peace, outer peace is impossible.

World peace cannot be achieved simply by wishing for external harmony while individual minds remain dominated by anger, hatred, fear and confusion.

Creating peace within our own mind affects the people around us—our family, our community and the wider society in which we live.

Our example matters. Wisdom matters. Compassion matters.

By creating peace in our own mind and helping others do the same, we contribute to peace in the world.

# Part IV — Closing Meditation

Sit comfortably and decide that, for a few minutes, you are going to meditate without following distractions.

Bring to mind a difficulty you are currently experiencing. It is better to begin with something manageable rather than your greatest difficulty.

Reflect slowly:

When things go wrong in my life and I encounter difficult situations, I normally regard the situation itself as my problem.

But the problem I experience depends greatly on my mind—on my view and my attitude.

Imagine responding to this situation with a positive and peaceful mind.

Imagine accepting the fact that it is happening.

The situation might then become a challenge, or even an opportunity for inner growth.

Think:

> This difficulty is helping me.  
> It is teaching me how to develop strength of mind and good inner qualities.

Remember that problems become more painful when the mind responds with anger, unhappiness or discouragement.

Come to the following conclusion:

> If I want to be happy and free from problems, and if I want to help others do the same, I must learn to develop and maintain a peaceful mind.

Hold this decision quietly for a while.

Then think:

> I can develop this peaceful mind through the way I respond to difficulties.  
> I can use them to develop humility, patience, wisdom, compassion and a deeper understanding of reality.  
> I will try to use the difficulties appearing in my life to improve myself for the benefit of others.

Finally, remember other people.

Think of the suffering that exists in your city, your country and throughout the world.

Develop the wish:

> How wonderful it would be if everyone could develop and maintain peace of mind and find the happy, meaningful life they long for.

Dedicate the benefit of the meditation to freedom from suffering and to peace in the world.

# Key Ideas at a Glance

- The real source of happiness is inner peace.
- Meditation is training the mind to become calm, peaceful and constructive.
- Difficult times are unavoidable; the crucial question is how we respond to them.
- Suffering can become meaningful when it is used for inner development.
- The same difficulty can be experienced very differently depending on our view.
- Difficulties can help us develop humility, patience, wisdom and compassion.
- Acceptance protects peace of mind; it does not mean that a difficult situation is good.
- The Buddhist teaching on karma encourages us to avoid harmful actions and cultivate beneficial ones.
- Our own suffering can remind us of the suffering of others and become a basis for compassion.
- Transforming adversity means using difficulty as material for spiritual development.
- Developing inner peace allows us to support others more effectively.
- According to the teaching, outer peace ultimately depends on the development of inner peace.
